# Research brief

**Question:** Does self-testing (retrieval practice) improve long-term retention more than rereading?

> **Needs human review:** Writer reported credible sources that contradict each other without explanation.

## Answer

The evidence generally supports that retrieval practice/self-testing improves long-term retention more than rereading or restudy in many settings [E1][E2][E3][E5][E6]. For example, repeated testing with feedback beat rereading at immediate, 18-day, and five-week delays in undergraduates learning introductory psychology concepts [E1], and a classroom meta-analysis found an average testing effect of g = 0.37 [E6]. However, the advantage is not universal: testing after worked-example study did not improve delayed problem-solving versus restudy across four experiments [E9], and two online Prolific experiments found no significant delayed-test advantage over restudy [E10].

## Key findings

- Repeated testing with feedback produced significantly better retention than rereading at immediate, 18-day, and five-week delays in an experiment with 83 undergraduate students learning introductory psychology concepts [E1].
- A review of applied classroom research concluded that retrieval practice is more advantageous for long-term retention than restudying the same information, though most reviewed studies compared retrieval to repeated study or no activity [E2].
- Practicing retrieval shortly after learning slows forgetting, and this benefit appears across material types, ages, learner abilities, and test types [E3].
- Laboratory studies in cognitive psychology show that repeated testing yields superior retention relative to repeated study, particularly when testing is spaced out over time [E5].
- A three-level meta-analysis of 53 classroom studies found an average testing effect of g = 0.37, moderated by knowledge type, posttest format, format matching between practice and test, and learning outcome type [E6][E7].
- Spacing retrieval allows more consolidated schemas to be reactivated, and feedback supports metacognitive monitoring of retrieval accuracy and shifts toward more effective retrieval strategies [E8].

## Limits and conflicting evidence

- Across four experiments with different initial test types, problem-solving domains, and participant populations, testing after studying worked examples did not improve delayed problem-solving performance compared to restudy [E9].
- In two online Prolific experiments using validated materials, delayed posttests, corrective feedback, and factual plus application measures, retrieval practice produced no significant advantage over restudy at delayed test [E10].
- The supporting classroom review notes that most reviewed studies compared retrieval practice to repeated study or to no activity [E2].
- The classroom meta-analysis found the testing effect was moderated by knowledge type, posttest format, format matching, and learning outcome type, while feedback provision and timing moderated the interleaving effect rather than the retrieval practice effect [E6][E7].
- These findings conflict with the generally supportive evidence for retrieval practice over rereading/restudy [E1][E2][E3][E5][E6].

## Open questions

- The evidence does not settle why retrieval practice failed to outperform restudy in worked-example problem-solving experiments [E9] or in online Prolific experiments [E10].
- It does not establish which specific task features, populations, or feedback conditions determine when retrieval practice beats rereading [E6][E7][E9][E10].
- It does not fully resolve whether the conflicting results reflect boundary conditions or implementation differences, because the evidence only reports differences in task, setting, and population [E9][E10].
